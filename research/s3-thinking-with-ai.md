Reading of the table. Only three studies **measure** learning or accuracy under randomisation: Bastani (school maths), Anthropic Jan 2026 (coding), Dell'Acqua (consulting accuracy). All three find the same shape — with the model, performance during the task goes up; **without it afterwards, or outside what it does well, performance goes down, and the user does not notice**. Everything else is self-report (Lee, Gerlich, Anthropic internal), tiny-N (Kosmyna, METR 2025) or pre-LLM theory (Bainbridge, Parasuraman). Horizons are one session to a few weeks; nobody has a multi-year deskilling result yet.

## 4. The teacher's claims against the evidence

**Claim 1 — "AI automates the basic data-analyst toolkit extremely well, so juniors risk being able to do only what AI does better and cheaper."** *Supported on the first half, partially on the second.* Support: the compression findings (Noy & Zhang −40% time and "inequalities are half-erased"; Peng: least-experienced "benefited the most"; Dell'Acqua: +43% for below-average vs +17% above) all say the tool raises the floor — the floor is the junior's market. Canaries: 22–25-year-olds in exposed occupations 19% below trend, through hiring, not firing; software developers 22–25 down "nearly 20%". Nuance the teacher must carry: (i) "data analyst" appears in none of the exposure rankings — the affected occupations named are developers and customer service; analyst-type occupations sit in the top quintiles but are not singled out; (ii) Humlum & Vestergaard find the same junior decline in Denmark but *not* in adopting firms — the cause is contested (interest rates, post-2021 over-hiring, education composition; Canaries itself says the pattern "attenuate[s] when controlling for education"); (iii) the effect is about three years old, US, one payroll provider; (iv) BLS still projects data-scientist employment +35% over 2025–35. Honest slide wording: "the entry rung is thinner, and the evidence is early".

**Claim 2 — "'I'll let AI do the code and move to higher-level thinking' fails: the model applies the formulas too, the human stops making mistakes, stops understanding, and ends up prompting and accepting."** *Supported as a mechanism, on short horizons.* Bastani: practice +48%, exam −17% — exactly "performance up, learning down"; the GPT Tutor arm (guardrails) removed the harm but added no gain. Anthropic Jan 2026: same shape in coding, −17 points, with the qualitative twist that the low scorers were the delegators and the high scorers asked for explanations. Dell'Acqua: outside the frontier, AI users were 19 pp *less* correct, and Bainbridge/Parasuraman predicted the mechanism forty years ago (skills that are not exercised decay; the better the automation, the less the operator checks). Lee et al.: the more you trust the tool, the less you evaluate it (β = −0.69, self-report). What the evidence does *not* say: that this is inevitable — the Anthropic high scorers used the same tool differently; Kalyuga's expertise reversal says a worked example is fine for a novice who studies it; Cunningham's position is that understanding becomes *more* valuable, not less. The failure is not "using AI" but delegating the part that would have been the learning.

**Claim 3 — "Where you set the dependence threshold determines lever vs crutch; students must navigate deliberately."** *Supported, and it is the only claim the literature converts into a rule.* Bastani's own word is "crutch"; Mollick's is "the effort is the point"; Karpathy's "not too bad for throwaway weekend projects" and Willison's "if I couldn't explain exactly what it does" are working thresholds; the Carpentries module says novices cannot judge generated solutions; Anthropic's engineers name the "paradox of supervision". Nuance: nobody has a validated threshold — the rules in §3 are practitioner heuristics plus two RCTs. Say so, then give one anyway (§5).

Cross-cutting honesty for the slide deck: productivity gains are real (Noy, Peng, Dell'Acqua inside the frontier; METR's own 2026 update says developers are "likely more sped up" now). The deskilling studies are one-session to one-week; the self-report studies (Lee, Gerlich, Anthropic internal) measure worry, not skill. No study yet shows multi-year deskilling from LLMs. The strongest honest sentence: *every randomised test so far shows the same asymmetry — the tool helps you now and the help disappears when the tool does, and you do not feel it disappearing.*

## 5. Proposed 15–20 min segment

**Threshold slide — one heuristic students can apply the same evening.**

> **Do it yourself first when** the task is one you are supposed to be *learning* (this semester's stats, this week's library) or one you could not check the output of. Write your answer/plan/first draft before you prompt (predict before you prompt).
> **Delegate when** you could do it yourself and are choosing not to — boilerplate, syntax you already know, translation between tools you already understand — and it is cheap to verify.
> **Always, before you accept:** (1) explain the output to someone in your own words; (2) ask "what would make this wrong?" and test one case; (3) if you cannot do (1), you have not delegated the task — you have delegated your understanding of it.
> Rule of thumb: *AI as tutor, not oracle* — ask it "why" and "what if" before "do".

Sources behind the slide: Bastani (crutch vs tutor), Anthropic Jan 2026 (conceptual-inquiry users learned and were fast), Crouch/Richland (predict first), Willison (explain-back), Carpentries 2026 ("novices are unable to assess the quality of a generated solution"), CS50 ("guide students toward solutions rather than offer them outright").

Homework hook (optional, fits the SPEC §3 deliverable): commit the `step1` prediction on paper as a photo or a `prediction.md` next to `step1-basic.json` — the git history then shows *when* they thought vs *when* they prompted.

## 6. Risks and what NOT to say

- **No brain-rot.** Kosmyna's authors ask explicitly: do not say "stupid", "dumb", "brain rot", "harm", "damage". The study is N=54, session 4 is 9 vs 9, EEG connectivity is within-task engagement, and it is still a preprint. If shown at all, show the quoting result (15/18 could not quote their own essay) with the N on the slide.
- **No "19% slower".** METR flagged its own headline "out of date" (2026-02-24); the follow-up CIs include zero and METR expects speed-ups now. The durable part is the perception gap (forecast +24%, believed +20% after, measured −19%) — use it as "you cannot feel it", not as "AI makes you slower".
- **No "AI will take your job".** Canaries is descriptive and says so; Denmark finds no firm-level link; BLS projects growth in data roles; Humlum's bound is ≤2% on earnings after two years. The honest claim is about the *entry rung* and the *floor*, not about the occupation.
- **No Toner-Rodgers.** The viral "AI-assisted scientists discover 44% more materials" paper (arXiv 2412.17866) was disowned by MIT on 2025-05-16 ("no confidence in the provenance, reliability or validity of the data"; https://economics.mit.edu/news/assuring-accurate-research-record). Useful as a 20-second aside on checking sources.
- **No moral panic about the tool itself.** Every RCT here also shows a productivity gain inside the frontier. Students who conclude "don't use it" have missed the point; the point is *sequencing* (predict, then prompt; understand, then accept).
- **Do not present self-report as measurement.** Lee (N=319), Gerlich (N=666), Anthropic internal (N=132) measure confidence and worry. Say "workers report" on those slides.
- **Do not cite Cunningham's posts as if readable**: all paywalled; use the free interview paraphrase and the titles only.
- **Do not quote the 2023 Dell'Acqua ">40% quality" without saying "working paper"**: the 2026 journal version dropped it.
- Keep the segment at 20 minutes: §3.3 and §3.4 are the technical spine of the session; this segment motivates §3.4's "you are the harness", it does not replace it.
